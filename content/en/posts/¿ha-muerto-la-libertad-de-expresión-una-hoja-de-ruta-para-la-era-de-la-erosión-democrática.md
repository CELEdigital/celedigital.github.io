On July 8, 2026, the Center for Studies on Freedom of Expression (CELE) and the Electronic Frontier Foundation (EFF) convened a global panel of experts, diplomats, and human rights advocates to discuss some of the most pressing issues regarding the state of freedom of expression around the world. As Agustina Del Campo, director of CELE, noted in her opening remarks, the fact that the conversation was originally scheduled as an in-person session at RightsCon—an event that was canceled due to foreign pressure on the Zambian government—reflects the fragile state of freedom of expression globally.

This roundtable marked one of Irene Khan’s final public appearances as the United Nations Special Rapporteur on Freedom of Opinion and Expression, whose term ended in July 2026. Joined by the Swedish Ambassador for Human Rights, Irina Schoulgin-Nyoni; the Head of Law and Policy at ARTICLE 19, Chantal Joris; and EFF Senior Counsel David Greene, the discussion—moderated by CELE researcher Nicolás Zara—began by analyzing current restrictions on freedom of expression based on national security, the protection of democracy, and child safety.

# On Freedom of Expression and National Security

Special Rapporteur Irene Khan began by contextualizing the state of freedom of expression worldwide: only 7% of the world’s population lives in liberal democracies, and many of these democracies are experiencing setbacks. This has led to the normalization of what she calls the “securitization” of freedom of expression: while authoritarian states have always used national security and counterterrorism laws to silence dissent, we are now seeing backsliding democracies employing those same tactics in Latin America, Asia, and—surprisingly—in Western Europe and North America, regions that have historically been champions of human rights and freedom of expression. She added that the world’s most powerful state has now “not only ignored international human rights law but has also retaliated against governments seeking to defend it.” As an example, he cited the United States’ retaliatory measures against Europe under the Digital Services Act (DSA), which sought to insulate the behavior of platforms based in its territory from the scope of that legal framework through tariffs, sanctions, and threats. She also pointed to the process of “commodification” of freedom of expression, whereby this right is treated as a bargaining chip, while tariffs are used to control the flow of expression.

Delving deeper into the securitization of freedom of expression, Chantal Joris (ARTICLE 19) noted that [the impact of armed conflicts on freedom of expression](https://www.article19.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Clearing-the-Fog-of-War-3-December-2024.pdf) extends beyond the parties and territories directly involved, citing as an example that discourse surrounding the situation in Israel and Palestine has been at the center of international censorship. This extends to the moderation of online content; for example, in the United Kingdom, where _Palestine Action_ has been designated a terrorist organization and the _Online Safety Act_ requires platforms to take action against terrorist content. He also mentioned how the Russian invasion of Ukraine led to a total ban on Sputnik and Russia Today, violating Europeans’ right to access information. Furthermore, he warned of the danger of extending the language of armed conflict to peacetime, noting that states increasingly speak of “hybrid warfare” and “psychological warfare” even in the absence of armed conflict, whenever they perceive an “information threat” emanating from a specific actor. This language of armed conflict, she added, is sometimes invoked to justify quite broad restrictions on freedom of expression.

Elaborating on the relationship between human rights and security, Ambassador Irina Schoulgin-Nyoni argued that, rather than controlling the debate, the best way to build a resilient citizenry is to ensure respect for freedom of expression and to help people regain trust in their societies. Regarding external threats, she noted that Sweden is training its public officials and—more importantly—raising awareness among its population to help them understand the forms that potential information manipulation might take. The Swedish approach to “psychological defense,” she added, is about civic responsibility and building a resilient democracy.

# On Age Verification and Design-Based Restrictions

EFF’s chief legal counsel, David Greene, addressed a different aspect of the rollback of freedom of expression. He explained that legislative efforts to restrict internet access based on users’ age can best be understood as an updated version of a long-standing authoritarian trend: the attempt to deny young people access to information. The democratic backslide in many countries, he added, makes this a dire moment to grant governments the mandate to restrict minors’ access to information, entertainment, and culture—and to deprive them of a platform to speak to the world. He noted that the technical aspects of age verification are important, but focusing solely on them means [giving up the fight for freedom of expression](https://www.techpolicy.press/they-said-id-feel-different-about-free-speech-as-a-parent-they-were-wrong/). The debate should not revolve around how we deprive young people of their rights, but rather whether it is a good idea to do so.

Greene also cautioned against excessive enthusiasm over recent jury verdicts in New Mexico and California that held social media companies civilly liable for harm caused to minors using their products. Each ruling is based on legal grounds that could threaten rights-protecting features, such as encrypted communications, and jeopardize protected speech. While he believes that some design features might be subject to less rigorous legal protection than online speech itself, he expressed skepticism about whether many of the efforts to regulate design were truly disconnected from concerns about online content; legislative debates on design almost always end up focusing on the speech of disadvantaged users. Certain core elements of speech are often characterized as design features, and restrictions on elements that could be separated from concerns about specific content (such as infinite scrolling or autoplay) would still have to meet a fairly high standard of scrutiny—at least an intermediate standard—under U.S. law.

# A Look to the Future

On a more encouraging note, Ambassador Schoulgin-Nyoni addressed the opportunities arising for Swedish leadership in addressing the issues outlined. She highlighted that approximately 30% of the budget of Swedish aid agencies is allocated to human rights and democracy. She emphasized that Sweden’s efforts are gaining momentum: given the magnitude of the challenges and the scarcity of resources, and as we see some large states distancing themselves from these issues, an increasing number of small and medium-sized countries have recognized the need to act jointly and in a complementary manner.

When discussing the similarities among the trends analyzed, Chantal Joris mentioned several advocacy points. First, she urged us to defend the right to free expression of those who disagree with us, especially in highly polarized environments. She also called on civil society experts to pay attention to nuances and not to be overly swayed by the “good intentions” behind regulations, focusing instead on chilling effects and the problematic aspects of technical solutions such as age verification. She also advocated for upholding international human rights law as a common language and for working at the national level to protect the rule of law and fundamental freedoms in order to foster trust in the international legal order. She concluded by highlighting some recent victories that have been replicated by various governments elsewhere—for example, regarding the use of _Palantir_ by state actors. She emphasized that freedom of information requests, investigative journalism, and effective awareness campaigns can still be effective.

Drawing on her [latest thematic report](https://www.srfreedex.org/freedom-of-expression-must-not-be-treated-as-fodder-for-geopolitics-or-commodity-for-trade-tariffs-says-un-expert/), Irene Khan addressed the collusion between governments and the oligarchs of big tech companies (_Big Tech_). She emphasized that, while international human rights standards have stood the test of time, the system that positions states as the primary guarantors of rights faces growing pressure due to the power imbalance between major platforms and most states. This is exacerbated when governments ally themselves with these companies to advance their own political agendas, allowing the platforms to profit while neglecting their human rights responsibilities. She added that the cost of this lack of accountability is borne primarily by women who are targeted, children who are vulnerable online, and journalists who lose their jobs because platforms profit from the fruits of their labor without compensation. To put human rights back at the center of the conversation, she proposed, first, the creation of broad, multi-stakeholder coalitions. Second, to hold governments accountable when they seek to legitimize prohibited discourse or ban legitimate expression, states must strengthen institutions that can act as checks and balances. Third, she referred to the need for states to break the monopoly of big tech companies and create an environment conducive to independent media and investigative journalism. Finally, he highlighted the importance of exploring new ideas on how to approach regulation and create alternative information spaces, especially in the Global South, where resources are most needed.
